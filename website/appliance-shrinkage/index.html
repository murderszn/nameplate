The Make-Ready Skim: Where Appliances Quietly Disappear

Every unit turn is an unsupervised inventory event. Here's how fridges get downgraded, warranties die unclaimed, and CapEx goes blind — and the Monday-morning fix.

8 MIN READ·FOR OPERATORS & INVESTORS·WITH ILLUSTRATIVE MATH

That $1,400 french-door refrigerator you bought in 2022? There is a meaningful chance it is not in unit 214 anymore — and nobody can prove where it went.

Not because your team is dishonest. Because the turn process makes it effortless: a dead fridge in a vacant unit, a working fridge in the unit being made ready next door, a 45-minute inspection window, and zero physical chain of custody. The swap solves today's problem and creates a $1,000 loss that surfaces eighteen months later as "premature failure."

Multiply that by every make-ready, every work order, every vendor visit — across every door you own. That is the skim.

01 / THE THREE LEAKSHow the money actually leaves

Leak one: the phantom swap. During turns, working appliances migrate toward the units that need to lease fastest. Donor parts — boards, handles, racks, elements — migrate the same way. Without a tag on the machine and a scan at the door, the ledger says everything is fine while the assets quietly downgrade.

Leak two: the dead warranty. A tech replaces a failed control board for $600. The board was under a five-year manufacturer warranty. Nobody knew, because the model number, serial number, and install date live on a corroded plate nobody can read — or in a spreadsheet nobody trusts. You just paid for a repair that was already guaranteed.

Leak three: blind CapEx. Ten water heaters installed the same month in 2017 are all dying the same summer. Without age visibility, each failure arrives as a midnight emergency instead of a planned Q2 replacement program. Emergency replacements cost more, lease worse, and wreck budgets.

Your CMMS knows about the work order. It does not know about the machine.

03 / WHY NOTHING CATCHES ITThe basement problem

Legacy property software was built for accountants in offices, not techs in utility closets:

  • No signal, no record. Basements and mechanical rooms kill cell coverage. Cloud-dependent apps fail exactly where the assets live.
  • Serial plates are unreadable. Heat, grease, and corrosion destroy manufacturer labels within years. Manual serial entry dies on contact with reality.
  • Tickets track labor, not lineage. "Replaced board" tells you what a tech did. It doesn't tell you which board, from which donor, under which warranty, in which unit.
  • Accounts are siloed. A fridge that walks across properties — or across operators — is invisible to every system that only sees one portfolio.

04 / THE WHITE-PAPER COREThe physical asset record

The fix is structural, not motivational: bind an immutable record to the machine itself. A tamper-evident tag with a unique ID goes on every appliance at install. Every subsequent event — install, turn audit, repair, part swap, warranty claim, retirement — is scanned against that ID and appended to an offline-first ledger that reconciles when signal returns.

STEP 01 — TAGOne tag per machine. Tamper-destructible plate with a unique ID, applied at install or during the next turn. The plate is the record's anchor.
STEP 02 — SCANSub-second verification in dead zones. The field app resolves the asset offline — no bars required — and walks the tech through a turn audit in minutes, not three quarters of an hour.
STEP 03 — LEDGEREvery event appends. Swaps, donor parts, warranty checks, and retirements accumulate into a per-asset service history no competitor — or departing vendor — can take with them.
STEP 04 — FORECASTHQ sees the fleet age in real time. Replacement liability by quarter, failure-prone brands, dead warranties resurrected — the board deck writes itself.

This is what Nameplate is: the tag, the offline field app, and the portfolio ledger. But the playbook below works even if you never talk to us.

05 / MONDAY-MORNING PLAYBOOKFive moves, no software required

  • Photograph every data plate at install. Model, serial, install date, unit. Store it where turns happen — not in a regional manager's inbox.
  • Make the turn audit an asset audit. Verify each major appliance against the unit's expected list before sign-off. Five minutes; catches 90% of drift.
  • Check warranty before you buy the part. One lookup — manufacturer warranty by model + install date — before every board, element, and compressor order.
  • Log donor parts. "Board from unit 118 → unit 214" on paper beats a perfect memory. Parts amnesia is how lemons hide.
  • Age your fleet once a quarter. A one-page list of every water heater, HVAC, and fridge past 80% of expected life. That page is your CapEx plan.
